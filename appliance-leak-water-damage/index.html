Appliance Leak Water Damage in Springfield: What to Know

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. A washing machine supply hose can split and run at full pressure until someone shuts it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. Behind a refrigerator, the thin tube feeding the ice maker may drip for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

Our Springfield crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. In kitchens that usually means under the cabinets and beneath the flooring. With a laundry leak we look at the rooms next to it and the wall behind the washer, plus the ceiling below when the laundry sits on an upper floor. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. When either one is wet, turn the power off at the breaker.
  2. Close the Supply ValveBehind a washer, on the wall, are separate hot and cold valves. The dishwasher valve is under the kitchen sink. A refrigerator line has a small valve below or behind the unit.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedDo not open a washer or dishwasher that is still full. The entire load of water may spill across the floor if the door is opened mid-cycle.
  5. Document What FailedKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. Your insurer and the appliance maker may both ask to see it.
Will Insurance Pay?

Insurance and Appliance Leak Water Damage

A hose that bursts or a machine that overflows without warning is the kind of sudden and accidental event homeowners policies often cover. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • A washer drain hose that slips out of the standpipe, or a clogged standpipe.
  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
  • Aging rubber supply hoses on a washing machine, which weaken and then split under pressure.
  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
  • Refrigerators that leak at floor level because the drain pan has cracked or the defrost drain is clogged.

Where the Damage Spreads

Floors and What Is Under Them

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. Boards cup or swell, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the surface looks dry.

Cabinet Damage

From below, a leaking dishwasher wets the toe kicks and the side panels of the cabinets. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

On a flooded kitchen or laundry floor, the baseboards and the lower edge of the drywall draw water upward. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Ceiling Below an Upstairs Laundry

In a home with an upstairs laundry, leak water passes through the floor and pools on the ceiling underneath. Sagging, staining and soaked insulation above the drywall are the usual result.

We apply antimicrobial treatment wherever discharge from the washer or the dishwasher made contact. Before the appliance is put back, a last set of readings confirms the floor under it is no longer damp.
